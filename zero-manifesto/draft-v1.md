# Someone should be paying attention.

*Why I'm building Zero.*

The wealthiest families in America don't have better information than you. They have someone paying attention.

Someone knows the vesting schedule. Someone has read the tax code. Someone notices the bonus landed on Tuesday and runs the numbers before it becomes a penalty. That attention compounds right alongside the money.

Everyone else gets a to-do list.

## I. Wealth buys attention.

Early in my career in private equity, I got a bonus. I checked that taxes had come out. I assumed the right amount had. When I filed, I owed a lot of money, plus a penalty for good measure.

I understood the problem the second I saw it. The expensive part was never the math. It was that nobody did the math in time.

That same year I had cash in a checking account that I knew belonged in Treasuries. It sat there. Not because I didn't know. Because it was on me to notice, and I was busy.

I hear versions of this every week. A friend had $2,000 sitting in commuter benefits. People pay 1% a year to own a fund that tracks the same index as the one that costs nothing. People learn they should have exercised their options the week after the window closes. My cofounder would have saved $20,000 in taxes by getting married 45 days later. He found out after we built the prototype.

Each one is manageable once someone points at it. The job is knowing what to point at, across everything you own, every year, forever.

The rich hire people for that job. It's the best thing money buys.

## II. You shouldn't have to know the question.

My parents knew how to build a business. In 2006 my father ran a loan origination company outside Detroit. Our income came from the mortgage market, and most of what we owned was tied up in the business.

After 2008, we lost our home.

Nobody ever sat with my parents while things were good and asked how much of our family's life rode on one market. Nobody asked whether some of it should live outside the business. I don't know what they would have decided. They should have been able to walk into that risk with their eyes open.

They didn't lack intelligence or effort. They lacked someone whose job it was to ask.

The most expensive financial decisions are usually the ones you didn't know you were making.

## III. Making advisors faster doesn't help you.

I spent five years at Alpine Investors, the last stretch leading AI across the portfolio. Two of those companies were a wealth management platform and an accounting platform. I saw the model from the inside, and I saw its ceiling.

Give an advisor AI and an hour of meeting prep becomes five minutes. The notes write themselves. The follow-up goes out faster. They take another meeting. The firm gets more productive.

Now ask what happened to the person whose bonus landed on Tuesday.

If catching it still depends on an advisor opening my file, noticing the deposit, and deciding to run the numbers, my financial life still runs on a busy person's attention. Doubling their client list doesn't fix that. It makes it worse.

Most of the industry is content with the bare minimum: a portfolio, a quarterly call, 1% a year. The bare minimum is worth paying for. It is also nowhere near what's possible now.

## IV. Point the intelligence at the household.

In college I spent a summer studying decentralized finance. We kept starting with the technology and hunting for a use. Even if you could move money for free, you still had to know what to do with it.

This time I know exactly where the intelligence should go: at what you earn, what you own, what you owe, and what you want.

When the bonus lands, work out what it does to your taxes and set the difference aside. Plan the tax bill in March, not April. Keep the portfolio where you said you wanted it. Know which options to exercise, and when, and what that does to AMT, cash, and concentration at the same time. Then carry it through, at a price that doesn't require you to be wealthy already.

That's Zero. A registered investment advisor built as software instead of staff. It sees your whole financial life, finds the moves you'd otherwise miss, and carries them out with your permission. It doesn't get busy. It doesn't wait for you to ask.

I'll judge it by what happens to a household over time. Did the cash get invested? Did the options get exercised? Did someone raise the concentration in the family business while things were still good?

## V.

Your financial position has always shaped what you feel allowed to want. It's permission to take the risk, or to take it with your eyes open. It's the trip you've put off for ten years. It's sleeping at night.

The wealthy have had someone paying attention for a very long time.

The rest of us are about to.
